Spatial pattern and interpretation

Spatial pattern. JRC classifies water only within a mapped area of about 11 km² of Srinagar’s 64.9 km², so surface water covers roughly 16% of the city (10.4 km² in 2021).

Temporal change. Permanent water fell from 5.37 km² (1991–93 average) to 3.83 km² (2019–21), a loss of 1.53 km² or 28.6%. The decline is steady rather than sudden: the linear trend is −0.061 km² per year (p < 0.001), and period means fall from 5.18 km² in 1991–2000 to 4.83 km² in 2001–10 and 4.05 km² in 2011–21. Seasonal water moved the opposite way, rising from 4.56 to 6.58 km² (+44.3%, +0.076 km² per year, p < 0.001). It peaked in 2016 at 7.04 km², the same year permanent water reached its minimum of 3.52 km². Total water changed little, from 9.92 to 10.41 km² (+4.9%), with a weak trend (+0.015 km² per year, p = 0.042, R² = 0.14). The main change is therefore in persistence rather than quantity: seasonal water grew from about 45% to about 63% of all water.

Transitions. Starting from 1991, the first well-observed year, 1.12 km² stayed permanent and 1.051 km² stayed seasonal. Permanent water that became seasonal was larger than seasonal water that became permanent, which agrees with the area trends above.

Reading the result carefully. The years 1990, 1995 and 1997 are poorly observed, with 80%, 28% and 95% of the mapped area unobserved, so their low values reflect missing data. They are excluded from all averages and trends, and the baseline starts in 1991. The shift from permanent to seasonal may reflect real hydrological change, but the classes also depend on how many valid observations fall within each year, so part of the shift could come from observation frequency or lake level rather than lasting loss of water. This dataset cannot separate these explanations, and causes such as land-use change or climate would need other evidence. The 30 m resolution also limits detail: a 0.49 km² change in total water is only about 540 pixels. The result applies to this boundary and ends in 2021. Overall, Srinagar’s surface water stayed near 10 km², but a growing share of it is seasonal.

Method, sources and limitations

The analysis selects the waterClass band from JRC/GSW1_4/YearlyHistory for 1990–2021, clips each year to one Srinagar boundary, and sums pixel area (30 m) for each class before converting to km². Every year uses the same colours. The share of the mapped water area with no valid observation is tracked for every year, and years in which it exceeds 20% are excluded from all averages and trends so that missing observations are not mistaken for change. The baseline is the average of the first three well-observed years, and it is compared with the last three (2019–21). The transition map starts from 1991 for the same reason.

Spatial resolution30 m pixels miss narrow channels and small ponds, and blur lake edges and floating vegetation.

Annual classesShort floods or dry spells are folded into one yearly label, so seasonal water mixes several processes.

No-data pixelsSnow, cloud and sparse early Landsat coverage leave pixels unobserved. Three years (1990, 1995 and 1997) fall above the 20% limit and are excluded.

Mapped extentJRC gives a class only inside the area where water has been detected, about 11 km² of the city’s 64.9 km². “Not water” therefore covers only a small part of the city.

Class definitions“Permanent” and “seasonal” follow JRC’s rules and may differ from local hydrological definitions. The dataset ends in 2021.

Fixed boundaryResults describe change inside one boundary and may not match the present legal city limit.

Boundary: Srinagar Municipal Corporation (ULB code 800013), from the Indian Urban Local Body boundaries . Water data: Pekel, J.-F., Cottam, A., Gorelick, N. and Belward, A.S.
